<objective>
Make the four entity tables answer "may this caller touch this row" in one place, and prove — before
accepting it — that a candidate still cannot touch another candidate's record.

**Purpose.** These four tables carry the phase's only self-access rules. Everywhere else in the estate a
policy asks about a *project*; here six clauses ask whether a row belongs to the caller, by comparing a
column to `auth.uid()`, and a seventh predicate exists to answer that same question and has never been
called. A3(a) folds all six into `user_can ('entity', id, <verb>)` and drops `is_candidate_self`. That is
the phase's thesis applied to the case that most obviously violates it.

**The problem this plan is built around: the dangerous failure is silent and it is one-directional.** Fold
a self-access rule in too strictly and a candidate cannot edit their own record — the E2E suite and
`02-candidate-self-edit.test.sql` say so at the first assertion. Fold it in too loosely and a candidate
edits *another* candidate's record, with every test still green, because a read test cannot tell "correctly
permitted" from "should have been denied". So the evidence is not that a candidate can edit their own row.
The evidence is a grid in which every table has a caller who may and a caller who may not, differing in
exactly one term, run first against two deliberately wrong predicates — one over-permissive, one
over-strict — with the reddened counts recorded.

**The denial that matters is the one nothing currently asserts.** `01-tenant-isolation.test.sql` proves a
caller of project A cannot see project B's entities. It does not prove that an entity grantee of
candidate A cannot reach candidate A2, *the same type, in the same project* — and that is precisely the
case a predicate written as `user_can ('project', project_id, …)` by mistake would permit while every
existing assertion stayed green. It is asserted here, per table, in both directions.

**D-21 is an instruction with a checkable consequence, and four identical tables are where it is
checkable.** Four entity tables is exactly where a hardcoded `candidate` would otherwise be written four
times. So the generalisation is made structural: with the table name and the entity-type literal replaced
by placeholders, the four policy expressions of each verb family are the *same string*. 162-14 inherits
that shape for storage; D-21's naming clause also retires the two policy names that carry an entity type
in their actor segment.

**Output.** Eighteen converted predicates in `302-rls.sql`; `is_candidate_self` absent from the declarative
schema and from `pg_proc`; one composition of the public-visibility rule rather than two, per Task 2's
ratified answer; a column bound for any self-update policy this plan creates; a new pgTAP grid file; the
existing estate given the grants claims that keep its assertions measuring what they say; a regenerated
`00001_initial_schema.sql` and `packages/supabase-types/`; and a wave-4 gate including the full E2E suite.
</objective>

## The design, settled before the first line of SQL

### The verb map — § 3.3, read for these four tables

Four rows of the matrix bear on entity tables. Their entity-column cells are what make the self-access
fold possible at all: an entity grant's reach is equality with the granted entity, so `own` needs no
column comparison.

| § 3.3 permission | Root / Account / ProjAdmin / ProjEditor | Candidate / OrgEditor / Faction / Alliance | Used by |
|---|---|---|---|
| `project.read_entities` | ✓ | — | the SELECT policies' project disjunct |
| `project.edit_entities` | ✓ | — | the INSERT, admin UPDATE and DELETE policies |
| `entity.read_answers` | ✓ | `own` | the SELECT policies' entity disjunct |
| `entity.edit_answers` | ✓ | `own` | the self-update policies |

**Note what the entity columns do *not* carry.** An entity grantee holds neither `project.read_entities`
nor `project.edit_entities`, so they reach no other entity in their project through a project-scope call —
which is exactly the same-type same-project denial this plan asserts. Their only path to another entity's
row is the public disjunct, and that is a property of the row, not of the caller.

### The eighteen predicates

`<T>` is the table, `<t>` its entity-type literal. Every expression keeps the house `(SELECT fn (…))`
wrapping so `auth.jwt()` is evaluated once per query.

| Policy | Verb | New expression |
|---|---|---|
| `authenticated_select_<T>` | `USING` | `(SELECT user_can ('project', project_id, 'project.read_entities')) OR (SELECT user_can ('entity', id, 'entity.read_answers')) OR <the public disjunct>` |
| `admin_insert_<T>` | `WITH CHECK` | `(SELECT user_can ('project', project_id, 'project.edit_entities'))` |
| `entity_update_own_<T>` | `USING` + `WITH CHECK` | `(SELECT user_can ('entity', id, 'entity.edit_answers'))` |
| `admin_update_<T>` | `USING` + `WITH CHECK` | `(SELECT user_can ('project', project_id, 'project.edit_entities'))` |
| `admin_delete_<T>` | `USING` | `(SELECT user_can ('project', project_id, 'project.edit_entities'))` |

**The six inline clauses and the three `has_role` clauses land in two of those rows and nowhere else.**
`authenticated_select_organizations`' `auth_user_id = auth.uid()` and its
`has_role ('organization', 'organization', id)` are the *same question asked twice*, and both become the
one `entity.read_answers` call. The same pair in `organization_update_own_organizations`, and the two lone
clauses in `candidate_update_own`, become the one `entity.edit_answers` call. Six clauses plus three role
calls collapse into four `user_can` calls across two tables — and the other two tables get the identical
expressions for the first time, because `user_can` can express for a faction what no column on `factions`
could.

### Where the parent's read lives — and why this plan does not branch on it

162-07b removed `authenticated_select_candidates`' organization-role disjunct and named this plan as where
that reach returns. It returns **inside `user_can`**, not as a policy disjunct: 162-04 Task 2 Q2 ratified
which permission `is_child_nominee` gates, and whichever letter was chosen, the predicate this plan writes
is the same `(SELECT user_can ('entity', id, 'entity.read_answers'))`. Under Q2 (A) that call answers true
for an organization grantee asking about its child candidate; under (D) or (B) it answers false and the
parent's reach is a `nominations` right that 162-12 implements. **The policy is identical; only the
assertion direction differs**, and that direction is read from `162-04-SUMMARY.md`.

This is the single-encoding rule working as intended: a plan that wrote the parent rule into a policy
disjunct would be re-deriving, inside a policy, a rule `user_can` already answers — and it would have to
guess Q2's answer to do it.

**One consequence is recorded rather than fixed here.** § 3.4's third row says the parent sees a related
entity's *basic data only, not answers unless public*. `answers` is a JSONB column on the entity row
(M6), and RLS is row-level, so a SELECT policy cannot admit the row while withholding the column. Under
Q2 (A) a parent organization therefore reads its child candidate's answers as a side effect of reading the
row. That is a column-grant question, `303-column-grants.sql` carries no SELECT grants at all today, and
the answer is 162-13's. **It is named in the SUMMARY as an open item, not silently accepted.**

### The public disjunct — one composition or two

§ 3.4's second row gives all project structure to any grantee; it is silent on what an authenticated user
with **no** grant in the project may read. Today the answer is `OR published = true` — the authenticated
policy carries the anon policy's own predicate, so `authenticated` is a superset of `anon`. M10 says that
disjunct is load-bearing for a real user, so it stays. The question is how it is *spelled*.

After 162-08 the anon entity policies are a composition: the project is open for voters, the entity's own
`confirmed` is true, a non-unconfirmed nomination in that project links it — and, for candidates, the two
terms-of-use terms. Writing that composition again inside four authenticated policies produces **eight**
copies of one rule. That is the parallel implementation this phase exists to end, and 162-17's structural
non-collapse guard is the instrument that would find it.

The alternative is one function — `entity_is_anon_visible (p_entity_type, p_entity_id)` — that composes
162-08's helpers once and is called by both halves. **Which policies call it is Task 2's Q1**, because the
widest form edits four predicates 162-08 proved with a grid, and the narrowest leaves two spellings of one
rule. The options and their costs are in the checkpoint; the recommendation is the widest, and 162-08's
own grid is the instrument that proves it behaviour-neutral.

### D-21, made structural

> *"This kind of generalisation should be applied as widely as possible."* — operator note on A4, recorded
> as D-21 and stated there as a phase-wide instruction.

Two consequences, both checkable:

1. **No predicate names an entity type where it could take one as an argument.** Read each policy
   expression from `pg_policies`, replace the table name with `TBL` and each of the four entity-type
   literals with `ENTITY`; for each verb family the four normalised strings must be **identical**. Under
   Q1's recommended answer this holds for all five families including SELECT, because the candidate-only
   terms-of-use conjuncts move inside the function that already takes the type as an argument. Under the
   narrower answers the SELECT family is identical only after the candidate-only terms are removed, which
   is a weaker claim and is stated as such.
2. **No policy NAME carries an entity type in its actor segment.** `162-PATTERNS.md` records the naming
   convention and D-21's amendment to it. `candidate_update_own` becomes `entity_update_own_candidates`
   and `organization_update_own_organizations` becomes `entity_update_own_organizations`. M7 says no
   assertion reads a policy name, so the rename costs two comment corrections.

**`admin_*` is deliberately left alone.** Under D-07 a ProjectEditor also holds `project.edit_entities`, so
the actor segment now reads as "the project-scope actor" rather than "an administrator". Renaming it would
cut across the identical names 162-09, 162-11 and 162-12 carry on their own tables, which is a phase-wide
convention question and not this plan's. It is flagged, not absorbed.

### The two tables with no self-update policy

§ 3.3 gives `entity.edit_answers` as `own` to Faction and Alliance grants, and D-07 maps FactionEditor and
AllianceEditor to real grant rows. No UPDATE policy on `factions` or `alliances` admits a non-admin today,
so those cells are currently unexercisable — the shape D-11a calls *"a permission nobody can exercise"*.
Adding the two policies makes all four tables one shape, which is what D-21's structural claim needs; it is
also a change in the permissive direction on tables that have never had one, and M6 says it requires a
column bound in the same commit or an entity editor can rewrite `project_id`. **Task 2's Q2 decides**, with
the column bound attached to the recommended answer rather than deferred.

### Where the matrix stops

`user_can` answers *may this role do this verb to this object*; it does not answer *is this object in a
state that admits the verb*. 162-04 Task 2 Q3 ratified that line. So `confirmed`, `open_for_voters` and the
terms-of-use timestamps are conjuncts of the **public disjunct**, never of the grant disjuncts — an entity
grantee reads and edits their own unconfirmed row, which is exactly what makes the sign-up flow possible.
Name immutability on a confirmed entity is a *trigger*, and it is 162-13's.
